One multiplier drives everything

Near the minimum the NLL is locally a quadratic bowl. Along each eigen-direction with curvature λ, a GD step just rescales the distance z from the optimum:

zₙ₊₁ = (1 − η·λ)·zₙ weights gₙ = λ·zₙ gradient Lₙ = ½·λ·zₙ² loss

Three readouts, one driver. The whole story is the multiplier r = 1 − η·λ, applied over and over.

The four regimes of η·λ

η·λ ≪ 1crawls — smooth but painfully slow decay
η·λ < 1contracts — z, g, L all head to zero
1 < η·λ < 2overshoots — flips sign every step; in a pure quadratic still decays geometrically
η·λ > 2quadratic: diverges · saturating: locks into a limit cycle

One η, many λᵢ: the same step size lands different directions in different regimes. The stiff direction is always the first to misbehave.

Why a real gradient plateaus — and a quadratic one can't

In a pure quadratic bowl the multiplier r = 1 − η·λ never changes, so ‖∇L‖ either decays geometrically (a straight line on the log chart) or explodes. A nonzero floor exists only on the knife edge η·λ = 2.

A real NLL's curvature relaxes away from W*. Overshoot too far → the effective step shrinks → pulled back in. Get too close → effective η·λ > 2 there → kicked back out. The bounce self-stabilizes into a fixed-amplitude limit cycle: loss sits at a tiny floor (it sees z²) while ‖∇L‖ plateaus at a genuinely nonzero value (it sees λ·z). Flip the bowl to saturating and hit stubborn — that's your lr=50 curve.

Two bowls: quadratic L(w) = ½(λ₁x² + λ₂y²) — the local Taylor model, constant curvature; and saturating L(w) = λ₁h(x) + λ₂h(y) with h(t) = √(1+t²) − 1 — same curvature λᵢ at W*, but it relaxes with distance, like a real NLL.
